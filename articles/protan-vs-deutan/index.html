Protan vs Deutan: What Is the Difference?

Updated 2026-10-28

Both are red-green color vision deficiencies and both are X-linked. Protan deficiency affects the red-sensitive L cones, so reds look darker and can approach black. Deutan deficiency affects the green-sensitive M cones, so reds and greens look similar but keep their normal brightness. Deutan is more common. Protan is more likely to make a red light look dim.

Updated 2026-10-28

Protan and deutan are the two halves of red-green color vision deficiency. They look similar on a plate test and feel similar in daily life. They are not the same thing, and the difference shows up in brightness before it shows up in hue.

Two cones, two names

The retina has three cone types, each tuned to a different part of the spectrum. L cones respond most to long wavelengths, which we call red. M cones respond to medium wavelengths, which we call green. S cones respond to short wavelengths, which we call blue.

Protan deficiency comes from the L cone. Deutan deficiency comes from the M cone. Both genes sit on the X chromosome, which is why both conditions are far more common in men: a man has one X chromosome, so one variant is enough. A woman needs the variant on both.

What each person actually sees

In protan deficiency, the red channel is weakened. Red objects look darker than they should, and a deep red can read as near-black or brown. That brightness loss is the part people notice in practice, because a red light at a distance loses its punch before a green light does.

In deutan deficiency, the green channel is weakened. Reds and greens drift toward each other and become hard to separate, but the brightness of a red object stays closer to normal. A person with deutan deficiency sees the confusion without the darkening.

Severity has its own vocabulary. A mild shift is an anomaly: protanomaly or deuteranomaly. A missing cone type is an opia: protanopia or deuteranopia. Most people with red-green deficiency have the anomaly form, which is why many pass through childhood without noticing anything.

Why the difference matters

Brightness is the safety issue. Traffic signals, brake lights, warning lamps on machinery and indicator LEDs all depend on a bright red being recognised as bright. A person with protan deficiency is more likely to underrate that brightness, particularly in low light or against a busy background.

Hue is the everyday issue. Choosing clothing, reading a map with color-coded routes, judging whether meat is cooked, or sorting wires all rely on separating red from green. Both deficiencies cause that confusion. Protan adds the brightness problem on top.

How a test tells them apart

A screening plate test can show that red-green confusion exists. It cannot reliably say which cone is involved, because both deficiencies make the same plates unreadable.

An anomaloscope separates them. You adjust a red-green mixture until it matches a yellow reference, and the match point differs in a characteristic way for protan and deutan. An arrangement test such as the Farnsworth D-15 shows the pattern of confusions instead. Both are clinical instruments, and both need a trained examiner.

What to do with the answer

If a screening test points at red-green deficiency, the useful next step is a clinical exam, not a purchase. A formal result tells you the type and the severity, and it is the document an employer or a licensing body will accept.
